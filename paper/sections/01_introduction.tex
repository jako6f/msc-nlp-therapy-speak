It is widely recognised that many countries are experiencing an unfolding mental health crisis. Evidence of escalating mental health complaints comes from surveys, epidemiological studies, and rising prescriptions of psychiatric medications \citep{haidtAnxiousGenerationHow2025}. Particularly substantial increases in prevalence have been reported for the neurodevelopmental disorders autism \citep{zeidanGlobalPrevalenceAutism2022} and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) \citep{cuiDSM5ChangesCOVID192026,mckechnieAttentiondeficitHyperactivityDisorder2023}. In Australia, for example, the number of people with autism increased by 42\% from 2018 to 2022, including a 95\% increase among women and girls \citep{abs2024}. In the United Kingdom, ADHD diagnoses increased by factors of two, three, and more than 10 for boys, girls, and adults, respectively, from 2000 to 2018 \citep{mckechnieAttentiondeficitHyperactivityDisorder2023}.

Concurrently, mental health awareness campaigns have proliferated \citep{bolinskiEffectEmentalHealth2020}, and mental health-related discourse has become increasingly common in everyday life \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,page18HowHarnessPower}. Again, ADHD and autism are particularly stark examples. In June 2026, ADHD and autism had been hashtagged in more than 5.4 million and 4 million videos on TikTok, respectively. An analysis of a LexisNexis dataset revealed that from January to May 2024, ADHD was the subject of 25,080 media articles, compared with 5775 articles during the equivalent period in 2014 \citep{martinChangingPrevalenceADHD2025}.

On the one hand, this popularisation of mental health related discourse in everyday life has lead to undeniably positive outcomes. It may give people language for subtle emotional states \citep{page18HowHarnessPower}, improve mental health literacy \citep{schomerusEvolutionPublicAttitudes2012}, and thereby reduce stigma around mental illness \citep{sampognaImpactSocialMarketing2017,flearyRelationshipHealthLiteracy2022}. It may also support better recognition and more accurate reporting of mental health problems \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023}, erode barriers to help-seeking that contribute to the ongoing under-treatment of some conditions \citep{haslamConceptCreepCalibration2026}, and facilitate online identity and community formation, particularly around ADHD and autism \citep{ginappExperiencesAdultsADHD2023}.

On the other hand, the concurrence of rising prevalence rates of mental ill health and the proliferation of mental health-related discourse in everyday life has prompted scholars to interrogate the relationship between the two \citep{haslamConceptCreepMental2026,foulkesAreMentalHealth2023}. \citet{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023} term this account the \textit{prevalence inflation hypothesis}, which posits a cyclical, mutually reinforcing dynamic. On one side of the cycle, rising prevalence may promote the permeation of mental health terminology into mainstream discourse. On the other, the increased availability of such discourse may itself inflate prevalence by encouraging overdiagnosis, overinterpretation, or pathologisation, whereby milder or more transient forms of distress are understood and reported as clinical mental health problems, often in conjunction with self-diagnosis \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,beekerPsychiatrizationSocietyConceptual2021,haslamConceptCreepMental2026}.

The mechanism is not merely linguistic. Once individuals interpret and label their psychological experiences as symptoms of a mental health condition, this may alter self-concept and behaviour \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,alperTikTokAlgorithmicallyMediated2025}. In stronger cases, such labelling may become self-fulfilling: ordinary distress, reframed as symptomatic of disorder, may lead to patterns of attention, avoidance, identification, or help-seeking that intensify the very symptoms being labelled \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023}. This process may be further amplified where mental health problems are glamorised or romanticised, that is, represented as socially desirable, identity-conferring, aesthetically meaningful, or markers of depth and authenticity. Under these conditions, diagnostic and quasi-diagnostic labels may acquire social value, making self-labelling attractive rather than merely explanatory \citep{ndourRomanticisationMentalHealth2025}.

These concerns are particularly relevant to ADHD and autism, where clinicians have reported increases in self-diagnosed presentations \citep{hartnettSocialMediaADHD2024,weigleSocialMediaYouth2024}. They may also be especially pronounced among adolescents, given their heightened susceptibility to rumination, peer influence, identity formation, social reward, and media messaging \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,ndourRomanticisationMentalHealth2025}.

Another corollary of the diffusion of mental health language into popular culture is a phenomenon described as \textit{therapy-speak}, ``the imprecise and superficial integration of psychotherapy language into everyday communication'' \citep{isern-masUnmaskingTherapyspeak2025}. The consequences are twofold. Firstly, therapy-speak may spread inaccurate psychological information. An analysis of the 100 most popular TikTok videos about ADHD found that more than half (55\%) of the characteristics attributed to ADHD by video creators did not align with the diagnostic criteria of the fifth revision of the \textit{Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders} (DSM-5) \citep{devriesExploringConceptCreep2025}. Similarly, an analysis of the 133 most-viewed TikTok videos tagged \#autism found that only 27\% were rated as accurate, 41\% as inaccurate, and 32\% as containing potentially misleading overgeneralisations \citep{aragon-guevaraReachAccuracyInformation2025}. Secondly, therapy-speak may erode the meaning and relevance of mental-health-related terms, a process that can also be understood as \textit{trivialisation}. This can contribute to hermeneutical injustice by depriving people who actually live with a certain mental condition of the words they need to describe their experiences \citep{isern-masUnmaskingTherapyspeak2025} and by reducing their symptoms to mere personality traits, thereby denying them a fully recognised psychiatric identity \citep{spencerIsntEveryoneLittle2021}.

This latter concern---the erosion of meaning in psychotherapy terms---is the focus of the present study, which investigates lexical semantic change (LSC) in the terms ADHD and autism in general web discourse. The following literature review first synthesises empirical evidence on LSC in mental-health-related terms over recent decades, before reviewing the computational approaches used to study these processes and identifying the gap addressed by this project.
