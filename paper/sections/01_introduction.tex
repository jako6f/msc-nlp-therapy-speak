Many countries appear to be experiencing an unfolding mental health crisis. Evidence of escalating mental health complaints comes from surveys, epidemiological studies, and rising prescriptions of psychiatric medications \citep{haidtAnxiousGenerationHow2025}. Particularly substantial increases in prevalence have been reported for the neurodevelopmental disorders autism \citep{zeidanGlobalPrevalenceAutism2022} and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) \citep{cuiDSM5ChangesCOVID192026,mckechnieAttentiondeficitHyperactivityDisorder2023}. In Australia, for example, the number of people with autism rose by 42\% between 2018 and 2022, including a 95\% increase among women and girls \citep{abs2024}. In the United Kingdom, ADHD diagnoses increased twofold among boys, threefold among girls, and more than tenfold among adults between 2000 and 2018 \citep{mckechnieAttentiondeficitHyperactivityDisorder2023}.

Concurrently, mental health awareness campaigns have proliferated \citep{bolinskiEffectEmentalHealth2020}, and mental-health-related discourse has become an increasingly ordinary feature of everyday life \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,page18HowHarnessPower}. Here too, ADHD and autism are particularly stark examples. By June 2026, ADHD and autism had been hashtagged in more than 5.4 million and 4 million TikTok videos, respectively, and an analysis of a LexisNexis dataset identified 25,080 media articles on ADHD between January and May 2024, compared with 5,775 in the equivalent period of 2014 \citep{martinChangingPrevalenceADHD2025}.

On the one hand, this popularisation of mental-health-related discourse has genuine benefits. It may give people language for subtle emotional states \citep{page18HowHarnessPower}, improve mental health literacy \citep{schomerusEvolutionPublicAttitudes2012}, and thereby reduce stigma around mental illness \citep{sampognaImpactSocialMarketing2017,flearyRelationshipHealthLiteracy2022}. It may also support better recognition and more accurate reporting of mental health problems \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023}, erode barriers to help-seeking that contribute to the ongoing under-treatment of some conditions \citep{haslamConceptCreepCalibration2026}, and facilitate online identity and community formation, particularly around ADHD and autism \citep{ginappExperiencesAdultsADHD2023}.

On the other hand, the concurrence of rising prevalence and proliferating discourse has prompted scholars to interrogate the relationship between the two \citep{haslamConceptCreepMental2026,foulkesAreMentalHealth2023}. \citet{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023} term this account the \textit{prevalence inflation hypothesis}, which posits a cyclical, mutually reinforcing dynamic: on one side of the cycle, rising prevalence may promote the permeation of mental health terminology into mainstream discourse; on the other, the increased availability of such discourse may itself inflate prevalence by encouraging overdiagnosis, overinterpretation, or pathologisation, whereby milder or more transient forms of distress come to be understood and reported as clinical mental health problems, often in conjunction with self-diagnosis \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,beekerPsychiatrizationSocietyConceptual2021,haslamConceptCreepMental2026}.

The mechanism is not merely linguistic. Once individuals interpret and label their psychological experiences as symptoms of a mental health condition, self-concept and behaviour may change in turn \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,alperTikTokAlgorithmicallyMediated2025}. In stronger cases, such labelling may become self-fulfilling: ordinary distress, reframed as symptomatic of disorder, may give rise to patterns of attention, avoidance, identification, or help-seeking that intensify the very symptoms being labelled \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,haslamConceptCreepMental2026}. This process may be further amplified where mental health problems are glamorised or romanticised---represented as socially desirable, identity-conferring, aesthetically meaningful, or as markers of depth and authenticity. Under these conditions, diagnostic and quasi-diagnostic labels may acquire social value, making self-labelling attractive rather than merely explanatory \citep{ndourRomanticisationMentalHealth2025}.

These concerns are particularly acute for ADHD and autism, for which clinicians have reported increases in self-diagnosed presentations \citep{hartnettSocialMediaADHD2024,weigleSocialMediaYouth2024}, and they may be especially pronounced among adolescents, given their heightened susceptibility to rumination, peer influence, identity formation, social reward, and media messaging \citep{foulkesAreMentalHealth2023,ndourRomanticisationMentalHealth2025}.

A further corollary of the diffusion of mental health language into popular culture is the phenomenon of \textit{therapy-speak}, ``the imprecise and superficial integration of psychotherapy language into everyday communication'' \citep{isern-masUnmaskingTherapyspeak2025}. Its consequences are twofold. First, therapy-speak may spread inaccurate psychological information. An analysis of the 100 most popular TikTok videos about ADHD found that more than half (55\%) of the characteristics attributed to ADHD by video creators did not align with the diagnostic criteria of the fifth revision of the \textit{Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders} (DSM-5) \citep{devriesExploringConceptCreep2025}; similarly, of the 133 most-viewed TikTok videos tagged \#autism, only 27\% were rated as accurate, 41\% as inaccurate, and 32\% as containing potentially misleading overgeneralisations \citep{aragon-guevaraReachAccuracyInformation2025}. Second, therapy-speak may erode the meaning and relevance of mental-health-related terms, a process that can also be understood as \textit{trivialisation}. This can contribute to hermeneutical injustice, depriving people who genuinely live with a condition of the words they need to describe their experiences \citep{isern-masUnmaskingTherapyspeak2025} and reducing their symptoms to mere personality traits, thereby denying them a fully recognised psychiatric identity \citep{spencerIsntEveryoneLittle2021}.

This latter concern---the erosion of meaning in psychotherapy terms---is the focus of the present study, which investigates lexical semantic change (LSC) in the terms ADHD and autism in general web discourse. The literature review that follows first synthesises empirical evidence on LSC in mental-health-related terms over recent decades, before reviewing the computational approaches used to study these processes and identifying the gaps addressed by this project.
